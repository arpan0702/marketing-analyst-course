Marketing Metrics, A/B Testing & Statistical Fundamentals

CAC, LTV, ROAS, hypothesis testing, confidence — where "analyst" stops being a title and starts being a way of thinking.

Beginner

Core concepts

  • CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) = spend ÷ new customers.
  • LTV (Lifetime Value) = avg. order value × purchase frequency × customer lifespan.
  • ROAS (Return on Ad Spend) = revenue ÷ spend. Healthy LTV : CAC is roughly 3:1 or better.
  • Statistical significance: whether an observed difference (e.g. Variant B's conversion rate) is likely real, or just noise.
  • Sample size / peeking risk: checking significance too early inflates false positives — a test that looks "significant" on day 3 can flip by day 14.

Intermediate

Sizing a test before you run it

Real teams don't check significance and hope — they calculate the sample size needed before launch, using industry-standard 80% power (chance of detecting a real effect if one exists) and 95% confidence (chance of not falsely calling noise a real effect). Skipping this is how teams end up running an underpowered test for two weeks and learning nothing.

Sample size calculator

Choosing the right statistical test, and reading cohorts over time

  • Chi-square test: comparing conversion rates (categorical: converted / didn't) between two groups — what this course's significance calculator runs under the hood.
  • t-test: comparing averages of a continuous number between two groups (e.g. average order value in Variant A vs. B) — different math from a rate comparison, a common trap in interviews.
  • Confidence intervals: instead of a single "the lift is 8%," a range ("the lift is between 3% and 13%, 95% confidence") — more honest, and what you should actually report to a stakeholder asking "so is it 8% for sure?"
  • Retention curves: the row-by-row read of Module 3's cohort heatmap — where retention typically drops steeply in month 1 then flattens; the flattening point is roughly your realistic long-run LTV assumption.

Advanced

Proving it's causal, not just correlated

  • Correlation vs. causation: spend and revenue moving together doesn't prove spend caused the revenue — both could be driven by seasonality. A recommendation based on correlation alone should be framed as a hypothesis to test, not a proven lever.
  • Incrementality / holdout testing: deliberately withhold a channel or campaign from a random slice of geography or audience, then compare outcomes vs. the exposed group — this is how you separate "channel gets credit for a sale" from "channel actually caused the sale," especially for brand/awareness spend attribution can't cleanly measure.
  • Multiple testing correction: testing 5 variants at a 5% significance threshold gives roughly a 1-in-4 chance at least one looks "significant" purely by chance. A simple fix (Bonferroni correction): divide your significance threshold by the number of comparisons (5 tests → use 0.01, not 0.05, as the bar).

Literacy note — not a tool to build, a skill to have

The next two topics (regression, clustering) are usually built by a data scientist, not a marketing analyst. But someone will hand you their output and expect you to evaluate it, not just nod. That's the bar here: read it well, don't build it from scratch.

Reading a regression someone hands you

Simple linear regression fits a straight line through a scatter of points (e.g. spend vs. revenue by campaign) to describe how one variable moves with another. You'll rarely build this yourself, but a data scientist or a vendor report will hand you output like this constantly — know how to read it.

what the line and the scatter tell you

TermWhat it tells you
R² (R-squared)0 to 1 — how much of the variation in revenue this model explains. 0.85 = strong fit; 0.15 = the line barely describes what's happening, treat the slope skeptically.
p-value (per variable)same logic as Module 5's significance calculator — below 0.05 means this variable's relationship is unlikely to be noise.
Slope / coefficientthe "for every +1 unit of X, Y moves by this much" number — the headline result, but only trustworthy if R² and p-value both back it up.
Multicollinearity (red flag)if two input variables are themselves highly correlated (e.g. spend and impressions), the model can't cleanly tell which one is doing the work — coefficients become unstable. If someone's regression has this problem, their individual coefficients aren't trustworthy even if overall R² looks fine.

Reading a cluster analysis someone hands you

Clustering is an algorithm that groups customers (or campaigns, or products) into buckets based on similarity across several numeric variables at once — more rigorous than a manual RFM-style bucketing, but the same underlying goal: find groups that behave alike. You'll typically receive "cluster 3 is your high-value, low-frequency segment" as a finished output, not build the algorithm.

What to check before trusting it: how many clusters were chosen (too few hides real differences, too many creates noise — there's rarely one "correct" number), what variables fed it (garbage in, garbage out — a clustering built only on spend will never surface a behavioral segment), and whether the clusters are stable (re-running it shouldn't reshuffle everyone into different groups).

Interview questions for this module

"How do you explain statistical significance to a stakeholder with no stats background?"

Model answer: "It's how confident we are the difference we're seeing is a real effect, not random noise from a small sample — like flipping a coin 10 times and getting 7 heads doesn't mean the coin is rigged, but 700 heads out of 1,000 flips would."

"If LTV:CAC is 1.5:1, what does that tell you and what would you recommend?"

Model answer: unsustainable — the cost of acquiring a customer is too close to what they're worth over their lifetime, leaving little margin for overhead or profit. Recommend either reducing CAC by channel (cut underperforming spend) or increasing retention/LTV (lifecycle marketing) before scaling acquisition spend further.

"How would you determine if you have enough sample size to trust an A/B test result?"

Model answer: run a sample-size calculation before launch based on baseline conversion rate and minimum detectable effect, and commit to that sample size and duration up front — don't stop the test early just because it looks significant on day 3, since that inflates the false-positive rate.

"What's the difference between correlation and causation, and how does it affect your recommendations?" (real, sourced)

Model answer: correlated metrics can share a third cause (seasonality driving both spend and revenue); recommendations from correlation alone should be framed as testable hypotheses, not proven levers — propose a holdout or A/B test before recommending a budget shift on correlation alone.

"You tested 5 variants and one came back significant — what's the concern?" (real, sourced)

Model answer: the multiple-comparisons problem — at a 5% threshold and 5 tests, there's roughly a 1-in-4 chance of at least one false positive by pure chance. Correct the threshold (Bonferroni: divide 0.05 by the number of tests) or treat the winner as a hypothesis to re-confirm.
